A master reference for product teams

Crafted Web Standard

How to make websites feel deliberate, useful, and authored rather than assembled from generic components. This guide covers the decisions that users actually feel: hierarchy, type, rhythm, motion, interaction feedback, content, and operational honesty.

Professional is not a style. It is the absence of unexplained decisions.Design operating principle

01 · The standard

Make fewer, stronger choices.

A site looks generic when every part is a familiar default: a huge gradient hero, rounded cards everywhere, a generic sans font, stock phrases, and motion with no job. A crafted site has a specific visual point of view and applies it consistently enough that it feels inevitable.

Generic output

Every section has a different decorative idea. Components look like they came from a kit. The headline promises more than the product delivers. Animation decorates instead of communicating.

Crafted output

One clear brand premise informs spacing, contrast, shape, photography, words, and motion. Every interaction answers a customer question or shortens a task.

Working test: If a decision could be swapped with any competitor’s design without changing the meaning, it is probably not a real design decision yet.
02 · Direction before screens

Write a visual brief before opening the editor.

Start with a one-page creative brief. This avoids design-by-accumulation and prevents a site from becoming a collage of current UI trends.

A

Name the customer’s dominant decision.

For a broad marketplace it may be “find a trustworthy option quickly.” For a premium consultancy it may be “decide whether these people understand my situation.” The most important decision determines density, imagery, proof, and CTA language.

B

Choose three brand adjectives and one tension.

Example: “quiet, exact, human” with the tension “clinical expertise without coldness.” A tension stops the work becoming a list of pleasant but generic traits.

C

Choose one hero material.

Use a real material from the product world: paper texture for a publisher, measured grids for engineering, close crop photography for food, actual work artifacts for services. Do not add decoration unrelated to the subject.

D

Define what the interface must never feel like.

Examples: “not a supermarket,” “not a fintech dashboard,” or “not a trend-led fashion drop.” Negative constraints are as valuable as inspiration.

03 · Typography

Typography is the fastest route to a distinctive feel.

Type does more than make words readable: it sets pace, authority, warmth, and memory. Choose roles, then make them consistent. Avoid relying on font size alone to create hierarchy.

Use a restrained pairing

Pair a display face with a workhorse text face only when the contrast adds meaning. A service brand may use a sober serif for outcomes and a neutral sans for evidence. A technical brand may use a clean grotesk plus tabular numerals for data.

Set a real scale

Define display, H1, H2, H3, body, label, and data styles. Assign weights, leading, max line-length, and letter spacing. Do not compensate for weak hierarchy by bolding everything.

Use line length deliberately

Keep body copy around 45–75 characters per line. Wider measures reduce comprehension; narrower measures create nervous rhythm. Use a separate width for compact utility copy.

Write for scanning

Headings must state an outcome, not merely a category. Use meaningful labels, short paragraphs, and proof near the claim it supports.

/* A minimal type system */
--font-display: Georgia, "Times New Roman", serif;
--font-body: ui-sans-serif, system-ui, -apple-system, "Segoe UI", sans-serif;
--text-body: clamp(1rem, .95rem + .15vw, 1.125rem);
--leading-body: 1.6;
--measure-reading: 68ch;
--tracking-label: .10em;
04 · Layout and rhythm

Use space to show what matters.

Polished interfaces have a spatial system. Density must match the customer task, not the designer’s preference: groceries benefit from compact repeatable rows; considered furniture needs room for dimensions and context.

DecisionCrafted defaultFailure to avoid
GridChoose a content grid and make exceptions intentional.Arbitrary widths and misaligned sections.
SpacingUse a small scale, e.g. 4/8/12/16/24/32/48/72.Random pixel values that make every section feel unrelated.
CardsUse only when a boundary is meaningful: selection, comparison, grouped status.Putting every paragraph in a rounded card.
ContrastUse contrast to establish hierarchy before using borders or shadows.Multiple shadows, strokes, badges, and colors competing at once.
ResponsiveRecompose information for narrow viewports, retaining essential actions.Merely shrinking desktop columns into unreadable mobile blocks.
Density rule: Let decision frequency set density. Frequent, low-risk decisions can be compact; expensive, personal, or irreversible choices deserve more context and space.
05 · Color and imagery

Color should clarify, not compensate.

Build a small semantic color system: foundations, surfaces, ink, muted text, primary action, and status colors. A palette becomes professional when it works across states, accessible contrast, images, and dark mode—not when it looks good as five swatches.

Choose one accent hierarchy

Reserve the strongest color for the highest-priority action. Offers, alerts, active states, and decorative marks must not all compete with it.

Use image direction, not image quantity

Define crop, light, distance, subject, background, and finish. A coherent visual brief is more valuable than a large photo carousel.

Give neutrals a temperature

Cool neutrals communicate precision; warm neutrals communicate tactility and hospitality. Pick deliberately rather than defaulting to grey.

Design all states

Specify hover, focus, selected, disabled, loading, success, warning, and error. State design is where polish becomes trust.

06 · Motion with a job

Motion should reveal cause and effect.

Good motion clarifies a change, protects orientation, or adds physical confidence. It should never delay content, hijack scrolling, or make a customer wait for an animation to finish.

01

Use entrance motion once, not everywhere.

One restrained page-load sequence can establish rhythm. Avoid every card rising and fading as it enters the viewport.

02

Animate continuity.

Cart confirmation, expanding filters, menu selection, and step transitions benefit from motion because it explains where the new state came from.

03

Keep timing quiet.

Use roughly 120–180ms for small feedback and 200–320ms for a panel or page-level transition. Use ease-out for arrivals; never use springy motion where precision or trust matters.

04

Respect reduced motion.

When prefers-reduced-motion is set, remove non-essential movement rather than merely slowing it down.

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  *, *::before, *::after {
    animation-duration: .01ms !important;
    transition-duration: .01ms !important;
    scroll-behavior: auto !important;
  }
}
07 · User experience

Remove uncertainty at the exact moment it appears.

Users judge the quality of a site by whether it answers the question they have now: what is this, will it fit, what does it cost, when will it arrive, what happens next, and what if something goes wrong?

Discovery

  • Use search when customers know what they want.
  • Use guided paths when product language is unfamiliar.
  • Keep taxonomy understandable without company jargon.

Selection

  • Make required choices explicit before the main action.
  • Show stock, compatibility, size, delivery, and terms before payment.
  • Compare only meaningful attributes.

Commitment

  • Keep checkout/booking steps short and named.
  • Show the complete price and next consequence.
  • Prevent errors before submission; explain them in place.

Aftercare

  • Make status, support, cancellation, and return options easy to find.
  • Use plain-language confirmation messages.
  • Never make support a scavenger hunt.
08 · Content and trust

Specific language makes a product feel real.

Generic copy is a major reason sites feel machine-made. Replace vague claims with useful details. Name the actual material, constraint, lead time, process, warranty, and outcome—only when true.

Weak

“Transform your workflow with our powerful, seamless solution.”

Useful

“Review purchase orders, flag missing vendor details, and export the approved file in one place.”

A

Use real proof.

Customer stories, outcomes, technical detail, policies, author names, and dated updates work because they can be checked. Do not fabricate social proof, scarcity, ratings, testimonials, or impact metrics.

B

Label commercial intent.

Mark sponsored content, affiliate links, promotions, optional insurance, recurring billing, and personalisation controls plainly. Trust is part of the interface.

09 · Build quality

Visual craft fails when the product is unreliable.

Performance, accessibility, security, and content stability are design inputs. A beautiful page that shifts while loading or loses a cart is not polished.

Performance

Prioritize the primary content and action. Use appropriately sized images, avoid unnecessary client JavaScript, reserve media dimensions, and test on a throttled mobile network.

Accessibility

Use native controls where possible, complete keyboard paths, meaningful focus states, form labels, alt text, semantic headings, and enough color contrast.

Resilience

Design empty, loading, error, offline, permission-denied, sold-out, and long-content states. They are normal product states, not edge cases.

Security and privacy

Collect the minimum data, show why it is needed, protect it at system boundaries, and never rely on client-side authorization or price calculations.

10 · Final review

Review the experience, not only the mockup.

Use this before release. Test the critical path in a real browser on desktop and a constrained mobile viewport. Invite someone unfamiliar with the product to complete the task without coaching.

  • The page has one dominant customer action.
  • The visual direction relates to the actual product, not a trend.
  • Type hierarchy works without color or images.
  • Every decorative element earns its space.
  • Buttons describe their outcome precisely.
  • All required choices are visible before commitment.
  • Loading, error, empty, and success states are designed.
  • Keyboard focus is visible and logical.
  • Text and control contrast meet WCAG AA.
  • Motion explains change and honors reduced-motion settings.
  • Mobile retains core context and controls.
  • Images have useful alternatives and reserved space.
  • Claims, urgency, reviews, and policies are truthful.
  • Performance is tested on slow mobile conditions.
  • No control promises a backend capability that does not exist.
A professional website does not need more decoration. It needs a coherent premise, purposeful interaction, high-quality content, operational truth, and the discipline to remove everything else.
